## Passage

> **22** He rose up that night, and took his two wives, and his two servants, and his eleven sons, and crossed over the ford of the Jabbok. **23** He took them, and sent them over the stream, and sent over that which he had. **24** Jacob was left alone, and wrestled with a man there until the breaking of the day. **25** When he saw that he didn't prevail against him, he touched the hollow of his thigh, and the hollow of Jacob's thigh was strained, as he wrestled. **26** The man said, "Let me go, for the day breaks." Jacob said, "I won't let you go, unless you bless me." **27** He said to him, "What is your name?" He said, "Jacob". **28** He said, "Your name will no longer be called Jacob, but Israel; for you have fought with God and with men, and have prevailed." **29** Jacob asked him, "Please tell me your name." He said, "Why is it that you ask what my name is?" He blessed him there. **30** Jacob called the name of the place Peniel: for, he said, "I have seen God face to face, and my life is preserved." **31** The sun rose on him as he passed over Peniel, and he limped because of his thigh. **32** Therefore the children of Israel don't eat the sinew of the hip, which is on the hollow of the thigh, to this day, because he touched the hollow of Jacob's thigh in the sinew of the hip.

## The Story

Jacob wrestles God on the worst night of his life, and comes out of it with a limp and a new name.

Twenty years earlier he fled home after cheating his brother Esau out of their father's blessing. He has spent those years in Haran working for his uncle Laban, who cheated him in return: swapped brides on his wedding night, changed his wages, kept him fourteen years for the wife he wanted. Jacob has left with two wives, two concubines, eleven sons, a daughter, and enormous flocks, and now God has told him to go home.

Home means Esau. Jacob sends messengers ahead and they come back with one piece of information: Esau is coming to meet him with four hundred men. Jacob does what Jacob does. He splits his people into two camps so at least one survives, sends waves of livestock ahead as gifts, and prays the first honest prayer of his life: "I am not worthy of the least of all the loving kindnesses ... Deliver me, please, from the hand of my brother." Then he sends his family across the Jabbok river and stays behind, alone, in the dark.

"And a man wrestled with him until daybreak."

The text does not explain who. It does not describe an attack, an argument, or a reason. One moment Jacob is alone; the next he is fighting a stranger, and the fight lasts all night. The Hebrew is full of wordplay: *Yabbok* (the river), *ye'abeq* (he wrestled), *Ya'aqob* (Jacob). This is the night Jacob was always going to have.

When the man sees he cannot pin Jacob, he touches his hip and dislocates it. A touch. The whole fight has been on the stranger's terms; he could have ended it any time. Even then Jacob will not let go. "Let me go, the day is breaking." "I won't, unless you bless me." Jacob has spent his life grabbing blessings by trickery, from his brother, from his blind father. Now, crippled and exhausted, he asks for one out loud.

The stranger's answer is a question: "What is your name?" The last time Jacob was asked that, by his father in the dark, he said "Esau." This time he says "Jacob": *heel-grabber*, *the one who trips people up*. And the stranger renames him. Israel, "he struggles with God," or "God fights." "For you have fought with God and with men, and have prevailed."

Jacob asks the stranger's name. He is refused, and blessed instead. Then the sun comes up and Jacob, who now calls the place Peniel, "face of God," limps across the river toward his brother. When he gets there, Esau runs to meet him, embraces him, and weeps. Jacob tells him, "I have seen your face, as one sees the face of God" (Genesis 33:10).

## What It Means

**God fought Jacob to bring him down, not to defeat him.** Jacob had spent his life prevailing by his own strength and cunning, and every victory cost him a relationship. The stranger lets the fight go all night, then wins it with a touch. The lesson is not that Jacob overpowered God; it is that God let Jacob exhaust himself and then blessed him anyway.

**The limp is the point.** Jacob walks away marked. The blessing he receives is inseparable from the injury; he cannot have Israel without the hip. Hosea 12:3-4 remembers the scene this way: "He struggled with the angel and prevailed; he wept and made supplication." Prevailing, in the Bible's memory, looked like weeping and begging.

**The new name is a new identity.** "Jacob" described how he had lived: grasping. "Israel" describes what he had done that night: held on to God and refused to let go. The nation carries that name. Its founding story is not a hero's triumph but a man who would not release his grip on God even after God had broken him.

**Who was the "man"?** The text calls him a man; Jacob calls him God; Hosea calls him an angel. Christian readers have often understood the wrestler as God appearing in human form, or as a preview of the incarnation: God coming close enough to be grabbed, and letting himself be held.

## Questions

### What does it mean that Jacob wrestled with God?

In Genesis 32:22-32, Jacob spends the night before meeting his estranged brother Esau wrestling an unnamed man who turns out to be God (Jacob names the place Peniel, "face of God," and says "I have seen God face to face"). The fight represents the turning point of Jacob's life: the schemer who grasped blessings by deceit finally clings to God directly, is wounded, and receives the blessing and the name Israel, "he struggles with God."

### Why did God touch Jacob's hip?

To end the fight and to leave a mark. The text says the man "saw that he didn't prevail against" Jacob, then touched his hip socket and dislocated it (Genesis 32:25). A touch was enough, which shows that the stranger had been holding back all night. Jacob left limping. The injury is a permanent reminder that the blessing he received came through being broken, not through winning.

### Why was Jacob's name changed to Israel?

Because of what happened that night. "Jacob" is linked to the Hebrew for "heel" and connotes grasping or supplanting, which is how he had lived. "Israel" means something like "he struggles with God" or "God fights," and the stranger explains it: "for you have fought with God and with men, and have prevailed" (Genesis 32:28). The name passed to his descendants and became the name of the nation.

### Who was the man Jacob wrestled with?

Genesis 32 calls him "a man," but Jacob says afterward, "I have seen God face to face," and Hosea 12:4 calls him an angel. Most Jewish and Christian interpreters understand him as God appearing in human form, or as the Angel of the LORD, a figure in Genesis who speaks and acts as God. The man refuses to give his name when Jacob asks.

### Where did Jacob wrestle with God?

At the ford of the Jabbok river, a tributary that flows into the Jordan from the east, in what is now Jordan. Jacob named the site Peniel (also spelled Penuel), meaning "face of God." He was on his way back to Canaan from Haran, and Esau was approaching from the south with four hundred men.

### What is the lesson from Jacob wrestling with God?

That God is willing to be held onto, and that the people he blesses are often the ones who refuse to let go even when they are hurting. Jacob's strength did not win the fight; his persistence after being injured did. The story also teaches that meeting God changes a person visibly. Jacob limped for the rest of his life, and Israel carried his new name.
