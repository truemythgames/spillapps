## Passage

> **10** Jacob went out from Beersheba, and went toward Haran. **11** He came to a certain place, and stayed there all night, because the sun had set. He took one of the stones of the place, and put it under his head, and lay down in that place to sleep. **12** He dreamed. Behold, a stairway set upon the earth, and its top reached to heaven. Behold, the angels of God ascending and descending on it. **13** Behold, Yahweh stood above it, and said, "I am Yahweh, the God of Abraham your father, and the God of Isaac. The land whereon you lie, to you will I give it, and to your offspring. **14** Your offspring will be as the dust of the earth, and you will spread abroad to the west, and to the east, and to the north, and to the south. In you and in your offspring will all the families of the earth be blessed. **15** Behold, I am with you, and will keep you, wherever you go, and will bring you again into this land. For I will not leave you, until I have done that which I have spoken of to you."
>
> **16** Jacob awakened out of his sleep, and he said, "Surely Yahweh is in this place, and I didn't know it." **17** He was afraid, and said, "How dreadful is this place! This is none other than God's house, and this is the gate of heaven." **18** Jacob rose up early in the morning, and took the stone that he had put under his head, and set it up for a pillar, and poured oil on its top. **19** He called the name of that place Bethel, but the name of the city was Luz at the first. **20** Jacob vowed a vow, saying, "If God will be with me, and will keep me in this way that I go, and will give me bread to eat, and clothing to put on, **21** so that I come again to my father's house in peace, and Yahweh will be my God, **22** then this stone, which I have set up for a pillar, will be God's house. Of all that you will give me I will surely give a tenth to you."

## The Story

Jacob's ladder is a dream a fugitive has on his first night away from home.

Jacob has just done two things that will define his life. He traded a bowl of stew for his twin brother Esau's birthright, and then, wearing Esau's clothes and goat skin on his arms, he tricked their blind father Isaac into giving him the blessing meant for the firstborn (Genesis 27). Esau's reaction was to plan a murder. Their mother Rebekah heard about it and sent Jacob north to her brother Laban in Haran, five hundred miles away, with the excuse that he should find a wife there.

So Jacob leaves Beersheba alone, on foot, with nothing. He is the grandson of Abraham, the heir of the promise, and he is sleeping in the open with a rock under his head because he cheated his way into that inheritance and now can't go home.

That is when he dreams. A stairway, or ladder, stands on the earth with its top in heaven, and angels are moving up and down it. The Hebrew word is *sullam*, used only here in the Bible; it probably describes something closer to a ramp or the stepped side of a ziggurat than a rope ladder. Above it stands God himself.

What God says is the point of the dream. He introduces himself as the God of Abraham and Isaac, and then repeats to Jacob, word for word, the promises he made to them: this land, descendants like the dust of the earth, blessing for every family on earth through his line. Then he adds something personal, because Jacob's situation needs it: *I am with you. I will keep you wherever you go. I will bring you back. I will not leave you until I have done what I said.*

None of this is conditional. God does not mention the stew, the goat skin, or the lie. He does not ask Jacob to make anything right first. He simply attaches himself to a man who is running from the wreckage of his own choices.

Jacob wakes up afraid. "Surely Yahweh is in this place, and I didn't know it." He has stumbled, by accident, onto a doorway between heaven and earth, and it turns out God has been standing at the top of it the whole time. He sets up the stone he slept on as a pillar, pours oil on it, and renames the place Bethel, "house of God." The old name was Luz.

Then he makes a vow, and the vow tells you exactly where Jacob is spiritually. *If* God keeps me, *if* he feeds me and clothes me, *if* he brings me home safe, *then* he will be my God and I will give him a tenth. God has just promised everything unconditionally; Jacob answers with a bargain. He is not there yet. He will spend twenty years with Laban being out-schemed by a better schemer before he comes back to this road, and to a night by the Jabbok river where God wrestles him into a new name.

But Bethel is where it starts. Years later, after everything, God will tell Jacob to go back and build an altar there (Genesis 35), and Jacob will describe it as "the God who answered me in the day of my distress, and was with me on the way that I went."

## What It Means

**The ladder goes down, not up.** Jacob does not climb it. Nobody does. The angels move on it, but the initiative is God's, who stands at the top and speaks first. The dream is not a picture of how to reach heaven; it is a picture of heaven reaching a man who is asleep on the ground.

**Grace arrives before repentance.** Jacob has done nothing to deserve this and everything to forfeit it. God's speech contains no rebuke. That is the same pattern that runs through the whole Bible: God commits first, and the change in the person comes afterward, sometimes decades afterward.

**"God's house" is wherever God shows up.** Luz was nothing, a spot on a road where the sun happened to set. Jacob's stone marks it as sacred because of who met him there, not because of anything about the place. The Bible keeps making this point, from the burning bush to the stable in Bethlehem.

**Jesus claimed the ladder.** When Nathanael first meets Jesus, Jesus tells him, "You will see heaven opened, and the angels of God ascending and descending on the Son of Man" (John 1:51). He is quoting Jacob's dream and putting himself in the place of the stairway. In Jesus' reading, he is the connection between heaven and earth that Jacob saw.

## Questions

### What is Jacob's ladder in the Bible?

Jacob's ladder is the stairway Jacob saw in a dream at Bethel, described in Genesis 28:10-22. It stood on the earth with its top reaching heaven, angels moved up and down it, and God stood above it and renewed to Jacob the promises he had made to Abraham and Isaac: land, countless descendants, and blessing for all nations. Jacob had the dream while fleeing his brother Esau, whom he had cheated out of their father's blessing.

### What does Jacob's ladder symbolize?

Jacob's ladder symbolizes God's connection to earth and his initiative in reaching people. The traffic on the stairway is angels, not humans; Jacob never climbs it. God stands at the top and speaks first, promising his presence to a man who is running from his own wrongdoing. In John 1:51 Jesus applies the image to himself, presenting himself as the link between heaven and earth that Jacob glimpsed.

### Was it a ladder or a stairway?

The Hebrew word *sullam* appears only once in the Bible, in this passage, so its exact meaning is uncertain. It is related to a root meaning "to heap up," and most scholars think it describes a ramp or stairway, possibly like the stepped sides of a Mesopotamian ziggurat, rather than a ladder with rungs. Older English translations chose "ladder"; many modern ones use "stairway."

### Where is Bethel?

Bethel was a town about 12 miles north of Jerusalem, at the site of the modern village of Beitin in the West Bank. Its earlier name was Luz. Abraham had built an altar near it (Genesis 12:8), Jacob renamed it after his dream, and it later became an important Israelite worship site, and eventually a center of idolatry under King Jeroboam (1 Kings 12).

### What does "climbing Jacob's ladder" mean spiritually?

The phrase comes from the African American spiritual "We Are Climbing Jacob's Ladder," where each rung represents progress in faith. It is a devotional use of the image rather than what the text says: in Genesis 28, Jacob does not climb anything. The ladder is God's route down to Jacob, not Jacob's route up to God.

### Why did Jacob pour oil on the stone?

Pouring oil was an act of consecration, setting something apart as holy. Jacob turned the stone he had slept on into a pillar marking the spot where God had spoken to him, and named the place Bethel, "house of God." He returned years later, at God's command, to build an altar there (Genesis 35:1-15).
