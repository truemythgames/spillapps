## Passage

> **25** Behold, a certain lawyer stood up and tested him, saying, “Teacher, what shall I do to inherit eternal life?” **26** He said to him, “What is written in the law? How do you read it?” **27** He answered, “You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, with all your soul, with all your strength, and with all your mind; and your neighbor as yourself.” **28** He said to him, “You have answered correctly. Do this, and you will live.” **29** But he, desiring to justify himself, asked Jesus, “Who is my neighbor?” **30** Jesus answered, “A certain man was going down from Jerusalem to Jericho, and he fell among robbers, who both stripped him and beat him, and departed, leaving him half dead. **31** By chance a certain priest was going down that way. When he saw him, he passed by on the other side. **32** In the same way a Levite also, when he came to the place, and saw him, passed by on the other side. **33** But a certain Samaritan, as he traveled, came where he was. When he saw him, he was moved with compassion, **34** came to him, and bound up his wounds, pouring on oil and wine. He set him on his own animal, and brought him to an inn, and took care of him. **35** On the next day, when he departed, he took out two denarii, and gave them to the host, and said to him, ‘Take care of him. Whatever you spend beyond that, I will repay you when I return.’ **36** Now which of these three do you think seemed to be a neighbor to him who fell among the robbers?” **37** He said, “He who showed mercy on him.” Then Jesus said to him, “Go and do likewise.”

## The Story

The Good Samaritan is not a children’s slogan. It is Jesus’ answer to a lawyer who wanted a smaller neighbor.

The man is a lawyer in the sense of a Torah expert. He stands up to test Jesus: what must I do to inherit eternal life? Jesus tosses it back — what is written? How do you read it? The lawyer nails the two great commands: love God (Deuteronomy 6:5) and love your neighbor (Leviticus 19:18). Jesus says, do this and you will live.

That should have ended it. The lawyer wants to justify himself. If “neighbor” can be defined tightly — kin, fellow Jew, the people who count — then he can check the box. So he asks the limiting question: who is my neighbor?

Jesus does not define the word. He tells a road story. Jerusalem to Jericho is a descent of about 3,300 feet in 17 miles, a known robber run. A man is stripped, beaten, left half dead. A priest comes by, sees him, and crosses to the other side. A Levite does the same. Both are religious professionals. Both see. Both pass.

Then a Samaritan. Jews and Samaritans in the first century did not share wells, worship, or much else (John 4:9). He is the last character the lawyer would pick as the hero. He sees, is moved with compassion, and then does a list: oil and wine on the wounds, his own animal, an inn, a night of care, two denarii — about two days’ wages — and an open tab when he comes back.

Jesus flips the lawyer’s question. Not “who counts as my neighbor?” but “which of these three *became* a neighbor?” The lawyer cannot even say “the Samaritan.” He says, “He who showed mercy.” Jesus: go and do likewise.

There is no hidden code in the innkeeper. The shock is the hero. The command is mercy that costs money, time, and the crossing of a line the lawyer wanted left in place.

## What It Means

**The parable answers a dodge.** “Who is my neighbor?” is how you shrink the second command. Jesus makes the neighbor the man in the ditch, and the neighbor-er the person you were trained to despise.

**Seeing is not mercy.** The priest and the Levite see. The Samaritan sees and is moved, then spends. Compassion that does not touch the body is the other side of the road.

**The hero is the outsider on purpose.** If the helper were a fellow Jew, the lawyer could keep his category. A Samaritan wrecks the category.

**“Go and do likewise” is the whole application.** Luke does not add a footnote. Eternal life was the opening question; the answer he is given is a road and an open tab.

## Questions

### What is the main message of Luke 10:25-37?

Love God and love your neighbor — and “neighbor” is whoever is in front of you in need, including the person your group does not count. A Torah expert asks how to inherit eternal life and how to limit the second command. Jesus tells the Good Samaritan parable and says, go and do likewise.

### What is the main lesson of the Good Samaritan?

Mercy is proven by what you do for the person who cannot pay you back, including the person you were told not to help. The priest and Levite are the warning. The Samaritan is the pattern.

### What is the hidden message in the Good Samaritan?

There isn’t a second secret plot. Some later sermons turn the inn into the church and the innkeeper into clergy. Luke’s own point is on the surface: the outsider is the neighbor, and the expert of the law is told to copy him.

### Who is my neighbor according to Jesus?

Whoever you have the power to help. Jesus refuses a membership list. He asks which traveler *became* a neighbor to the man who was beaten, and the answer is the one who showed mercy.

### Is the Good Samaritan a real story?

It is a parable — a story Jesus made up to answer the lawyer. The road is real. The hatred between Jews and Samaritans was real. The man in the ditch is a character, not a named historical victim.
