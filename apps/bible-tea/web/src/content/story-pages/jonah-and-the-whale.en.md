## Passage

Then the sea will be calm for you; for I know that because of me this great storm is on you." **13** Nevertheless the men rowed hard to get them back to the land; but they could not, for the sea grew more and more stormy against them. **14** Therefore they cried to Yahweh, and said, "We beg you, Yahweh, we beg you, don't let us die for this man's life, and don't lay on us innocent blood; for you, Yahweh, have done as it pleased you." **15** So they took up Jonah, and threw him into the sea; and the sea ceased its raging. **16** Then the men feared Yahweh exceedingly; and they offered a sacrifice to Yahweh, and made vows. **17** Yahweh prepared a great fish to swallow up Jonah, and Jonah was in the belly of the fish three days and three nights.

I will pay that which I have vowed. Salvation belongs to Yahweh." **10** Then Yahweh spoke to the fish, and it vomited out Jonah on the dry land.

## The Story

Jonah and the whale is really the story of a prophet who did not want his enemies forgiven, and the fish is the least strange thing in it.

Jonah, son of Amittai, is a real prophet from the northern kingdom of Israel, mentioned in 2 Kings 14:25 in the reign of Jeroboam II. God tells him to go to Nineveh, the great city of Assyria, and preach against it. Assyria is the empire that will, within a few decades, wipe Israel off the map. Its cruelty was proverbial. Jonah's response is to go to the port of Joppa and buy passage to Tarshish, probably in Spain, the far western edge of the known world, in the exact opposite direction.

The book tells you why only at the very end. Jonah did not run because he was afraid of Nineveh. He ran because he was afraid God would forgive it.

God sends a storm. The pagan sailors pray to their gods and throw cargo overboard while Jonah sleeps in the hold. When the lots identify him, he tells them the truth: he is fleeing the God who made the sea. His solution is to be thrown in. The sailors, who owe him nothing, row for shore instead, and only when that fails do they pray to Yahweh for forgiveness and throw him over. The sea goes flat. Everyone on that ship ends up worshipping Israel's God except Israel's prophet.

Then the fish. The Hebrew says *dag gadol*, "great fish." It never says whale; that came from the Greek word *ketos* in Matthew 12:40, which means any large sea creature. God "prepared" it, which is the book's way of saying that even the rescue is on his schedule. Jonah spends three days and three nights inside it.

Chapter 2 is his prayer, and it is a psalm of thanksgiving, not a confession. Jonah describes going down: into the sea, under the waves, weeds wrapped around his head, to "the bottoms of the mountains," to the gates of Sheol. Then the turn: "You brought up my life from the pit." He ends with the line the whole book pivots on: "Salvation belongs to Yahweh." He means it about himself. He has not yet worked out that it applies to Nineveh too. God speaks to the fish, and it deposits Jonah on dry land.

The second half of the book is the part people forget. Jonah goes to Nineveh and preaches the shortest sermon in the Bible: "Forty more days and Nineveh will be overthrown." The city repents from the king down. God relents.

And Jonah is furious. Chapter 4 opens with him praying the most honest prayer in Scripture: *I knew this would happen. I knew you were gracious and merciful and slow to anger. That's why I ran.* He sits outside the city and waits for it to burn anyway. God grows a plant to shade him, then kills it with a worm, and Jonah says he would rather die than live without his plant. God's final question is the last line of the book: you care this much about a plant you did not grow. Should I not care about a city of a hundred and twenty thousand people?

Jonah never answers. The book ends there, with the question hanging over the reader.

## What It Means

**The book is about God's mercy toward outsiders.** Every non-Israelite in the story, the sailors and then the Ninevites, responds to God faster and better than his own prophet. Jonah is the only character who knows God well and the only one who resents what God is like.

**You cannot outrun God, and he does not stop calling.** Jonah goes down to Joppa, down into the ship, down into the hold, down into the sea, down to the roots of the mountains. God meets him at the bottom, and the second chapter is proof that the lowest place a person can go is still within reach of prayer.

**Jesus made the fish the sign of his own resurrection.** When religious leaders demanded a sign, Jesus said the only one they would get was "the sign of the prophet Jonah": "as Jonah was three days and three nights in the belly of the whale, so will the Son of Man be three days and three nights in the heart of the earth" (Matthew 12:39-40). Jonah's descent and return is treated as a preview of Easter.

**Repentance is for the prophet too.** Nineveh repents in chapter 3. Jonah never does, at least not in the text. The book's real invitation is not to be like the sailors or the Ninevites, but to notice how much of Jonah is in the reader, and to answer the question God leaves open.

## Questions

### What is the Bible verse about Jonah and the whale?

Jonah 1:17: "Yahweh prepared a great fish to swallow up Jonah, and Jonah was in the belly of the fish three days and three nights." The Hebrew calls it a "great fish," not a whale. The word "whale" entered English Bibles through Matthew 12:40, where the Greek *ketos* means any large sea creature. Jonah 2:10 records his release: "Yahweh spoke to the fish, and it vomited out Jonah on the dry land."

### What is the story of Jonah and the whale in summary?

God told the prophet Jonah to preach in Nineveh, the capital of Israel's enemy Assyria. Jonah fled by ship in the opposite direction. God sent a storm; the sailors threw Jonah overboard and a great fish swallowed him. After three days of prayer inside it, he was spat onto land. He preached in Nineveh, the city repented, and God spared it. Jonah was angry at the mercy shown to his enemies, and God confronted him about it.

### What is the moral of the story of Jonah and the whale?

That God's mercy extends to people we would rather see judged, and that running from him does not work. Jonah fled not because he feared Nineveh but because he feared God would forgive it. The book ends with God asking Jonah, and the reader, whether they can accept a God who has compassion on their enemies. Jonah never answers; the question is left for us.

### What did Jesus say about Jonah and the whale?

Jesus called Jonah's three days in the fish "the sign of the prophet Jonah" and said it pointed to his own death and resurrection: "as Jonah was three days and three nights in the belly of the whale, so will the Son of Man be three days and three nights in the heart of the earth" (Matthew 12:40). He also said the people of Nineveh, who repented at Jonah's preaching, would condemn his own generation for not repenting at his (Matthew 12:41; Luke 11:29-32).

### Was it a whale or a fish?

The Hebrew text of Jonah says "great fish" (*dag gadol*). Matthew 12:40 uses the Greek *ketos*, a general term for a sea monster or large sea creature, which the King James Version translated "whale." The Bible does not identify the species, and the text presents the fish as something God specifically "prepared" for the moment.

### How many times did God tell Jonah to go to Nineveh?

Twice. The first command is in Jonah 1:2, which Jonah fled from. The second is in Jonah 3:1-2, after he was released from the fish: "The word of Yahweh came to Jonah the second time, saying, 'Arise, go to Nineveh, that great city, and preach to it the message that I give you.'" This time he went.
