## Passage

> **2** When Yahweh spoke at first by Hosea, Yahweh said to Hosea, "Go, take for yourself a wife of prostitution and children of unfaithfulness; for the land commits great adultery, forsaking Yahweh." **3** So he went and took Gomer the daughter of Diblaim; and she conceived, and bore him a son. **4** Yahweh said to him, "Call his name Jezreel; for yet a little while, and I will avenge the blood of Jezreel on the house of Jehu, and will cause the kingdom of the house of Israel to cease. **5** It will happen in that day that I will break the bow of Israel in the valley of Jezreel." **6** She conceived again, and bore a daughter. Then he said to him, "Call her name Lo-Ruhamah; for I will no longer have mercy on the house of Israel, that I should in any way pardon them. **7** But I will have mercy on the house of Judah, and will save them by Yahweh their God, and will not save them by bow, sword, battle, horses, or horsemen." **8** Now when she had weaned Lo-Ruhamah, she conceived, and bore a son. **9** He said, "Call his name Lo-Ammi; for you are not my people, and I will not be yours."

> **1** Yahweh said to me, "Go again, love a woman loved by another, and an adulteress, even as Yahweh loves the children of Israel, though they turn to other gods, and love cakes of raisins." **2** So I bought her for myself for fifteen pieces of silver and a homer and a half of barley. **3** I said to her, "You shall stay with me many days. You shall not play the prostitute, and you shall not be with any other man. I will also be so toward you." **4** For the children of Israel shall live many days without king, and without prince, and without sacrifice, and without sacred stone, and without ephod or idols. **5** Afterward the children of Israel shall return, and seek Yahweh their God, and David their king, and shall come with trembling to Yahweh and to his blessings in the last days.

## The Story

Hosea and Gomer is the story of a prophet whose marriage was his message.

Hosea preached in the northern kingdom of Israel in the eighth century BC, during the last decades before Assyria destroyed it. The country was prosperous and religiously busy, and almost none of that religion was directed at God. Israel worshipped Baal on the hilltops, credited him for the harvests, and treated the covenant with Yahweh as one option among many. The prophets called this adultery, because the covenant at Sinai had been a marriage, and Israel had gone looking for other lovers.

God's first word to Hosea is not a sermon to preach. It is an instruction about his life: marry a promiscuous woman and have children with her. Whether Gomer was already unfaithful when he married her or became so afterward is debated, but the text is blunt about what she was: a "wife of prostitution." Hosea, a prophet of a holy God, is told to bind himself to her, so that when he stands up to speak about Israel, the whole town will know he is describing his own house.

The children come, and God names them. The first son is Jezreel, after the valley where the ruling dynasty had seized power in a massacre: a name that means judgment is coming. The daughter is Lo-Ruhamah, "not pitied." The third child is Lo-Ammi, "not my people." Imagine calling those names across a courtyard. Every time Hosea speaks to his own children he is announcing that God's patience with Israel has run out.

Then Gomer leaves. Chapter 2 describes it through the lens of Israel: she chases lovers who she thinks give her bread and wool and oil, not realizing that everything she had came from the husband she walked out on. She ends up destitute. By chapter 3 she belongs to someone else, whether as a slave or a debtor or simply another man's woman.

And God speaks again: "Go again, love a woman loved by another, and an adulteress." Not *find her*. Not *take her back*. *Love* her. Hosea goes and buys her: fifteen shekels of silver and about ten bushels of barley, roughly the price of a slave. He pays for his own wife. Then he brings her home and tells her she will live with him, apart from other men, for a long time, and he will do the same for her.

Hosea does not get a happy ending written down. We are not told whether Gomer stayed faithful, or what their marriage looked like afterward. What we get instead is the book's closing chapters, where God speaks to Israel in the voice of a husband who cannot let go: "How can I give you up, Ephraim? ... My heart is turned within me, my compassion is aroused" (Hosea 11:8). The last chapter promises healing, return, and blossoming "like the lily." The prophet's story stops; the love it was illustrating does not.

## What It Means

**God chose to be understood through a broken marriage.** He could have used any image for Israel's idolatry. He chose the one that hurts the most, and he made his prophet live inside it, so that the anger in the book would sound like a husband's grief rather than a judge's verdict.

**The children's names are reversed by the end.** In Hosea 1:10 to 2:1 and again in 2:23, God promises that "not my people" will be called "my people," and "not pitied" will be called "pitied." Paul quotes these verses in Romans 9:25-26 to describe Gentiles being brought into God's family. The names that announced judgment were always going to be undone.

**Love here is a decision, not a feeling.** "Go again, love a woman loved by another." Hosea is commanded to love someone who has given him every reason not to, and the love takes the form of a purchase, a price paid for someone who cannot pay it herself. Christians have always read the fifteen shekels as a shadow of a larger redemption.

**Faithfulness is expected in return.** Hosea's terms in 3:3 are not sentimental: stay with me, no other men, and I will be faithful to you. Grace in this book is not the absence of standards. It is the refusal to give up on the person who failed them.

## Questions

### Who were Hosea and Gomer in the Bible?

Hosea was a prophet in the northern kingdom of Israel in the eighth century BC, and the author of the Book of Hosea. Gomer, daughter of Diblaim, was the woman God told him to marry, described as "a wife of prostitution." Their marriage, her unfaithfulness, and Hosea's decision to buy her back were a living illustration of Israel's idolatry and God's refusal to abandon his people.

### Why did God tell Hosea to marry Gomer?

So that Hosea's life would show what Israel had done to God. The covenant between God and Israel is described throughout the Old Testament as a marriage, and Israel's worship of other gods as adultery. By marrying an unfaithful woman and staying with her, Hosea gave the people a picture they could not ignore: God was the betrayed husband, and he had not stopped loving them (Hosea 1:2; 3:1).

### Did Hosea really love Gomer?

The text says he was commanded to, and that he acted on it: "Go again, love a woman loved by another, and an adulteress. So I bought her for myself" (Hosea 3:1-2). He paid a real price to bring her home and set terms for a faithful life together. The Bible does not describe his feelings, but it does describe his actions, which is how the Bible usually defines love.

### How many children did Hosea and Gomer have?

Three. A son named Jezreel, meaning "God scatters," after the valley where Israel's ruling dynasty had come to power by bloodshed; a daughter named Lo-Ruhamah, "not pitied"; and a son named Lo-Ammi, "not my people" (Hosea 1:3-9). Each name was a message about God's coming judgment on Israel, and each is reversed in God's promises later in the book.

### Why did Gomer leave Hosea?

Hosea 2 describes her, as a picture of Israel, chasing "lovers" she believed provided for her, not recognizing that her food, clothing, and security came from her husband. The chapter portrays her pursuit of other men as the same thing as Israel's pursuit of Baal: a search for provision from someone other than the one who had actually been providing it.

### Did Hosea buy Gomer back?

Yes. Hosea 3:2 says he bought her for fifteen shekels of silver and a homer and a half of barley. The combined price was roughly the value of a slave, which suggests Gomer had fallen into slavery or debt bondage. Hosea paid to redeem his own wife and brought her home.
