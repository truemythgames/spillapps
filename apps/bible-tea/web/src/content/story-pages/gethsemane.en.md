## Passage

> **36** Then Jesus came with them to a place called Gethsemane, and said to his disciples, "Sit here, while I go there and pray." **37** He took with him Peter and the two sons of Zebedee, and began to be sorrowful and severely troubled. **38** Then he said to them, "My soul is exceedingly sorrowful, even to death. Stay here, and watch with me." **39** He went forward a little, fell on his face, and prayed, saying, "My Father, if it is possible, let this cup pass away from me; nevertheless, not what I desire, but what you desire."
>
> **40** He came to the disciples, and found them sleeping, and said to Peter, "What, couldn't you watch with me for one hour? **41** Watch and pray, that you don't enter into temptation. The spirit indeed is willing, but the flesh is weak." **42** Again, a second time he went away, and prayed, saying, "My Father, if this cup can't pass away from me unless I drink it, your desire be done." **43** He came again and found them sleeping, for their eyes were heavy. **44** He left them again, went away, and prayed a third time, saying the same words. **45** Then he came to his disciples, and said to them, "Sleep on now, and take your rest. Behold, the hour is at hand, and the Son of Man is betrayed into the hands of sinners. **46** Arise, let's be going. Behold, he who betrays me is at hand."

## The Story

Gethsemane is the hour Jesus could have walked away, and did not.

It is late on Thursday of Passover week. The last supper is over. Judas has left the table and gone to the priests. Jesus has told the eleven that they will all fall away that night, and that Peter will deny him three times before the rooster crows. They have sung a hymn and walked out of Jerusalem, down into the Kidron Valley, and up the western slope of the Mount of Olives, to a walled olive grove Jesus used so often that Judas already knew where to find him (John 18:2). The name of the place is Gethsemane: *gat shemanim*, "oil press." Olives were crushed here.

He tells eight of the disciples to sit. He takes the inner three — Peter, and James and John, the two sons of Zebedee — a little farther in. These are the same three who saw him raise Jairus's daughter and who saw him transfigured on the mountain. Now they see him come apart. Matthew says he "began to be sorrowful and severely troubled." Jesus does not hide it. "My soul is exceedingly sorrowful, even to death. Stay here, and watch with me."

He goes a stone's throw beyond them, falls on his face, and prays the prayer the church has never gotten over: "My Father, if it is possible, let this cup pass away from me; nevertheless, not what I desire, but what you desire."

The cup is not a metaphor he invented on the spot. In the prophets, the cup is what God hands the nations when judgment arrives — "the cup of the wine of wrath" (Jeremiah 25:15; see Isaiah 51:17, Psalm 75:8). Jesus is not asking to skip an inconvenient arrest. He is asking whether there is any other way for the world's sin to be dealt with than for him to drink that cup himself.

He comes back. The three men he asked to stay awake are asleep. He speaks to Peter, the one who had sworn he would die before he denied him: "What, couldn't you watch with me for one hour? Watch and pray, that you don't enter into temptation. The spirit indeed is willing, but the flesh is weak."

He goes again. The second prayer has shifted. He is no longer asking whether the cup can be avoided. "My Father, if this cup can't pass away from me unless I drink it, your desire be done." He comes back. They are asleep again. Their eyes are heavy. Luke, telling the same night, adds that they were sleeping "from sorrow" (Luke 22:45), and that Jesus' sweat became like drops of blood falling to the ground, and that an angel appeared to strengthen him (Luke 22:43-44). Mark records the first word of the prayer as *Abba*, Father (Mark 14:36).

A third time he prays the same words. Then he wakes them for good. "Sleep on now, and take your rest. Behold, the hour is at hand, and the Son of Man is betrayed into the hands of sinners. Arise, let's be going. Behold, he who betrays me is at hand."

He does not run the other way down the mount. He walks toward the torches.

## What It Means

Matthew frames Jesus' public life with two tests. In the wilderness at the start (Matthew 4), Satan offers him the kingdoms without the cross. In the garden at the end, the test is the opposite: take the cup, or leave it. Both times Jesus answers with the Father's will. Gethsemane is not Jesus discovering, late, that he does not want to die. It is the Son, in a human body, telling the truth about what obedience is going to cost, and choosing it anyway.

**The cup is wrath, not inconvenience.** People sometimes read "let this cup pass" as a request to avoid pain. The Bible's cup language is heavier than that. Jesus is about to become, in Paul's words, "sin on our behalf" (2 Corinthians 5:21) and "a curse for us" (Galatians 3:13). Hebrews remembers this night as the moment he "offered up prayers and petitions with loud crying and tears" and "learned obedience" through what he suffered (Hebrews 5:7-8). The sorrow "even to death" is the weight of that exchange beginning to land.

**Honest prayer and surrendered prayer are the same prayer.** He asks for what he wants. Then he places what he wants under what the Father wants. The second request is not a cancellation of the first. It is what happens when a real desire meets a trusted Father. That is why this scene became the pattern under the line he had already taught them to pray: "your will be done" (Matthew 6:10).

**The disciples fail at the one thing he asked.** He did not ask them to understand the cup. He asked them to stay awake and pray so they would not collapse when the hour came. They could not do it for one hour. Hours later Peter will swing a sword, then deny him. Gethsemane is the preview: willing spirit, weak flesh. The warning is for anyone who thinks resolve is the same as readiness.

**He finishes the prayer before the arrest starts.** Judas is already on the path. Jesus gets up on his own. The next scene — the kiss, the swords, the ear, the healing — belongs to the next page. This page ends with a man who has decided, standing up, and walking toward the people coming to take him.

## Questions

### What is the explanation of Matthew 26:36-46?

Matthew 26:36-46 is the account of Jesus praying in the garden of Gethsemane on the night before the crucifixion. After the last supper he takes the disciples to an olive grove on the Mount of Olives, brings Peter, James, and John farther in, and three times asks the Father to let "this cup" pass — the cup of God's judgment — then submits: "not what I desire, but what you desire." The disciples fall asleep each time he returns. The passage ends as Judas approaches and Jesus goes to meet him.

### What lessons can we learn from Gethsemane?

That obedience can include telling God you do not want the thing he is asking, and then doing it. That prayer is how Jesus faced the worst night of his life, not how he escaped it. That friends you love may fail you at the exact moment you need them, and the work still has to be done. And that a willing spirit is not enough if the body and the will are not trained by watching and praying.

### Why did Jesus pray three times in Gethsemane?

Matthew says he "prayed a third time, saying the same words" (Matthew 26:44). The repetition is the point. He does not get a different answer. He stays in the request until the request has changed him: from "if it is possible, let this cup pass" to "if this cup can't pass unless I drink it, your desire be done." Persistent prayer here is not bargaining. It is remaining with the Father until he is ready to stand up.

### What does Gethsemane mean?

Gethsemane comes from the Aramaic *gat shemanim*, "oil press" or "olive press." It was an olive grove with a press on the western slope of the Mount of Olives, across the Kidron Valley from the Temple. The name fits the scene: olives were crushed there for oil, and this is where Jesus is pressed by the cup he is about to drink.

### What is the cup Jesus asked to pass from him?

In the Old Testament the cup is a picture of God's judgment — "the cup of the wine of wrath" that the nations are made to drink (Jeremiah 25:15; Isaiah 51:17; Psalm 75:8). Jesus is not asking only to avoid arrest or nails. He is asking whether the wrath due to sin must fall on him. The rest of the New Testament says that it did (2 Corinthians 5:21; Galatians 3:13; 1 John 4:10).

### Where is the Garden of Gethsemane?

On the Mount of Olives, just east of Jerusalem's Old City, across the Kidron Valley from the Temple Mount. John says Jesus went there often (John 18:1-2). The traditional site, still planted with ancient olive trees, sits beside the Church of All Nations. The exact first-century grove cannot be pinned to a single modern fence line, but the slope and the name have not moved.

### Why were the disciples sleeping in Gethsemane?

Matthew says "their eyes were heavy" (Matthew 26:43). Luke adds they were sleeping "from sorrow" (Luke 22:45). It was late, after a long Passover meal, and they did not understand what the hour was. Jesus treats the sleep as failure, not as a joke: they could not watch one hour, and they were about to enter the temptation he had told them to pray against.
